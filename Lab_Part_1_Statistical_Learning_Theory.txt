2.Explain how you would structure the development of your predictive model to maximize generalization.
a.Describe the key steps in your model development process that help ensure good generalization.
Data Preparation: Handle missing values (fill numericals with median, categoricals with mode), cap outliers, encode categorical variables, standardize numerical features, and split the data 80/20 using stratified sampling.
Model Selection: Start with logistic regression for its interpretability and also test Random Forest to capture non-linear relationships. Analyze feature importance (e.g., low watch hours or many complaints may indicate churn).
Validation & Optimization: Use k-fold cross-validation with metrics like accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. Optimize with regularization (L1/L2) for logistic regression or tune Random Forest parameters, then select the best model.
Deployment & Monitoring: Deploy the chosen model for real-time churn prediction, monitor performance over time, and update it as customer behavior shifts. Use insights to drive targeted retention strategies.

3.Provide a real-world example of how failing to achieve good generalization might negatively impact the business.
a.Consider the potential business consequences of deploying a churn prediction model that performs well on historical data but poorly on new data.
A churn model that doesn’t generalize wastes retention budget on the wrong customers, fails to save the right ones, distorts revenue forecasts, and damages trust in data-driven decisions, all while appearing successful on paper.
b.How could poor generalization affect decision-making, customer retention strategies, and overall business performance?
Poor generalization turns a model from an asset into a liability. It leads decision-makers to act on false confidence, causes retention budgets to be spent on the wrong customers with the wrong offers, and ultimately raises costs, lowers revenue, and weakens trust in data-driven strategy.
